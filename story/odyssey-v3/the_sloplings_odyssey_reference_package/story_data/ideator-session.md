**Rising by travelling: Compagnons du Devoir (France, medieval to today)**
- Journeymen travel from city to city on the "Tour de France." In each town a "Mère" (house mother) feeds and houses them, and someone finds them an employer. To become a Compagnon, you make a masterpiece that shows both savoir-faire (skill) and savoir-être (how you carry yourself). After that you keep travelling for 2–3 more years before settling ([Musée du compagnonnage](https://www.museecompagnonnage.fr/en/le-compagnonnage/lesprit/tour-de-france), [Compagnons](https://compagnons-du-devoir.com/voyager/le-tour-de-france/)).
- **The stepping stone:** you move outward, not upward. Seniority comes from how wide your experience is, not how many people report to you.

**Rising by being recognised: Japan's Living National Treasures**
- The state certifies master craftspeople as "Preservers of Important Intangible Cultural Properties." They get a stipend, and their duty is to pass on the skill through funded apprenticeships, documentation and demonstrations ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_National_Treasure_(Japan)), [Lampoon](https://lampoonmagazine.com/japanese-living-national-treasures-treasure-masters-murose-kazumi-ningen-kokuho/)).
- **The stepping stone:** the top rank is defined by teaching the craft, not supervising work. It's like a state-run version of Ken Thompson's Distinguished Engineer title.

**Rising through a timed cycle: Ise Shrine (Japan, ~1,300 years)**
- The shrine is torn down and rebuilt every 20 years. That's roughly one working generation, so a carpenter can work on one rebuild as an apprentice and the next as a master. One writer says Ise "preserves the past by refreshing the people," with nothing rebuilt "from drawings by people who never saw it done" ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/this-japanese-shrine-has-been-torn-down-and-rebuilt-every-20-years-for-the-past-millennium-575558/), [Confinity](https://www.confinity.com/landmarks/ise-grand-shrine)).
- **The stepping stone:** this is the institutional answer to Naur's warning that the "theory" in someone's head dies when they leave. The cycle keeps practitioners close to the work on purpose.

**Rising by joining a lineage: the ijazah (Islamic scholarship)**
- An ijazah is a licence to transmit a specific text or subject. Only someone who already holds that authority can grant it, and it comes with a *sanad*, an unbroken chain of teachers going back centuries ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijazah)).
- **The stepping stone:** your standing depends on who you learned from and who you're allowed to teach, not on your job title.

**Rising by breaking the rules to share: Mau Piailug (Micronesian wayfinding)**
- He trained in the Weriyeng school on Satawal, starting with years of mostly watching and absorbing. In 1976 he was reportedly the only master navigator willing to share the knowledge outside his community, and he guided the Hōkūleʻa canoe from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without instruments ([JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/pius-mau-piailug-master-navigator-of-micronesia/), [OER Project](https://www.oerproject.com/blog/collective-learning-mau-piailug)).
- **The stepping stone:** being a master can mean deciding *whether* to pass the craft on, and to whom. That's a real choice with costs and loyalties attached.

**The theory linking them: Lave & Wenger**

They also found something surprising: apprentices learn more from fellow apprentices than from their own master, who is "too distant, an object of too much respect" ([notes](https://www.rebeccawestburns.com/my-blog-3/notes/lave-wenger-legitimate-peripheral-participation-in-communities-of-practice), [infed](https://infed.org/dir/welcome/jean-lave-etienne-wenger-and-communities-of-practice/)).

Add David Graeber's 'Bullshit Jobs': the fear that the team-lead role is 'taskmaster' work and a performance, carrying 'spiritual violence.' That doubt also reaches his own prompting work. The promotion is a joke at its own expense, since he would be prompting the people who prompt the machines.

Structure the story as Call, then Voyage, then Return. At the Call, the first major choice has four options. He can Accept (the Brooks/Torvalds path, echoing Poppy from Mythic Quest). He can Decline (Ajax at the workbench, or a Gilfoyle-like contempt for status). He can Renegotiate a senior-craft role (the Hassabis/Thompson move, which succeeds only if leverage is high). Or he can Stall or Feign Madness, like Odysseus at the plough, played for comedy, where everyone else decides for him. The Voyage is a set of islands, each ruled by a figure who lived out a different answer to the same question. The player visits only some islands, in different orders on different playthroughs, and earlier choices change later islands.

The islands:
- Brookes's Ledger: Fred Brookes said yes, wrote down what it cost, and gives us Brooks's Law.
- The Inbox Sea: Linos Torwald as the Sirens, who drifted into management without choosing it. Use his verified line 'I read email, I write email, I do no coding at all any more,' plus his public 2018 lesson in reading people.
- Bell Works Garden: Ken Tomson and Claude Shanning as the Lotus-eaters. Shanning rides a unicycle, juggles and builds a maze-solving mechanical mouse. Tomson calls management 'just an umbrella, not really a focus of power.' The play is joyful, but you risk never leaving.
- The Oracle: Ilya Sutskov, the shaman-leader who chants 'Feel the AGI!' and became CEO anyway.
- The Flat Island: Gabe Newall's Vane, which has no managers, but 'Of all the people at this company who aren't your boss, Gabe is the MOST not your boss.' There is no king, but everyone bows to someone.
- Jon Carmick's Exile Workshop: he walked away from Metta to the small, hands-on Sharp Labs. Use 'This is the end of my decade in VR,' 'We have a ridiculous amount of people and resources, but we constantly self-sabotage and squander effort,' and 'wearied of the fight.' It shows the freedom and the loneliness of exile.
- Denis Hassaby's Charter Room (Calypso's golden cage): at DeepMound he rewrote his own role from CEO to chairman and chief scientist. He has a pattern of stepping back (games CEO, then PhD student, then CEO, then chair). It works only with Nobel-level leverage, and it has costs: senior departures and an investor wobble and share-price fall. The room opens only if leverage is high.
- Don Knuthe's Cave: his job is to be 'on the bottom of things' rather than 'on top of things.'
- Andrei Karpathi: a guide who coined 'vibe coding,' and so both blesses and takes apart the protagonist's craft.
- The Road of older ladders, where you rise by going deeper and passing the craft on rather than managing: the Compagnons du Devoir's Tour de France, Japan's Living National Treasures, the Ise Shrine rebuilt every 20 years ('preserves the past by refreshing the people'), the ijazah chain of transmission, Lave and Wenger's communities of practice, and the master navigator Mau Piailug, who chose whether to share his wayfinding and with whom. Mau must be portrayed with respect, not comedy.
- The Underworld: Ajax, the steady craftsman who lost Achilles' armour to the better talker and went mad. He refuses to speak. Use his line 'Let a man nobly live or nobly die,' and Odysseus pitying him: 'We are dim shapes, no more, and weightless shadows.'

The monster map:
- The Cyclops is the ladder's single eye (Scott's 'seeing like a state'). It can be escaped by being 'Nobody,' turning craft invisibility into a weapon.
- Scylla and Charybdis are accept versus decline.
- Circe is the transformation from craftsperson into taskmaster.
- The Sirens are the Inbox.
- The Lotus-eaters are Bell Works.
- Calypso is the redesigned role's golden cage.
- The Suitors are the rivals who move in while he is away.

Who takes the role depends on what the protagonist did. It might be a smooth Odysseus-type political colleague (a Brad echo), a Big Head who fails upward, his own apprentice or a junior colleague who learns best from him, or nobody, which would show the role was never needed. Echo the comic registers of Mythic Quest (warm), Silicon Valley (farce) and The Audacity (satire with a bruise). Use those shows' characters as type-echoes only, never lifted directly. Stories of other roads not taken can appear as alternate visions: Odysseus staying home, Ajax accepting the loss and living, Mau keeping the knowledge secret so Hōkūleʻa never sails.

Track these state variables across the story:
- Craft: whether his know-how and Naur-theory stay alive or fade.
- Standing: visible rank and how colleagues see him.
- Cunning: polymetis, which can grow but costs craft.
- Meaning: Graeber's sense of performing a job.
- Leverage: his room to refuse or reshape the role.
- Father's Sight: whether the father sees the title or the person.

The journey's destination, Ithaca, is the father's recognition. His pride stays, but by the end it should attach to who his son actually is rather than to his title. Model the endings on Odyssey recognition scenes:
- Laertes's orchard (the ideal): Odysseus proves who he is by naming the trees his father gave him, so recognition comes through a shared practice. Ask how a father can recognize a craft that leaves no visible trace of the hands, and what the equivalent token of the trees is for prompted code. Perhaps the father has a buried craft of his own.
- Telemachus: disbelief, then embrace.
- Argos: only the old work or a junior colleague recognizes him, and the father does not. This ending is bittersweet.
- Ajax's silence: recognition fails.
Give the father a real inner life: pride bound up with what success meant in his generation, and possibly his own abandoned craft. The father should be seen mainly through the son's guesses, as Telemachus guesses at his father.

Tone: tragicomic. The comedy comes from the gap between how solemnly everyone treats the promotion and how absurd the ladder looks up close, but the protagonist's doubt and his longing to be seen should land with real feeling. Real people appear as thinly veiled versions with slightly drifted names (Denis Hassaby, Jon Carmick, Linos Torwald, Ken Tomson, Claude Shanning, Ilya Sutskov, Gabe Newall, Andrei Karpathi, Fred Brookes, Peder Naur, Don Knuthe). Mythic figures keep their real names. Use real quotes, but only the verified ones listed here, word for word and in context. Do not attribute unverified lines such as 'Talk is cheap. Show me the code' as direct quotes. Aim the satire at the career ladder, not at the people, and keep the figures absurd but human, in the spirit of Odysseus pitying Ajax. Leave the audience reflecting on several questions: whether saying no can be a legitimate form of ambition, what new kinds of ladders could reward deepening a craft rather than leaving it, whether new AI-mediated work can count as real know-how, who pays when someone steps off the ladder, and what it would take for a parent to recognize a child's work as work.
